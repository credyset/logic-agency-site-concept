Retailer onboarding requirements go beyond having a good product — buyers evaluate category fit, margin structure, velocity proof, operational readiness, and marketing support before placing a first PO. The brands that get to shelf aren't always the best products. They're the ones that made the buyer's job easy.

After 20+ years working with brands through the retail onboarding process — and sitting on the other side of that table — we've seen what separates a brand that gets a first meeting from a brand that gets a first PO. This is the checklist that retail buyers actually work from, whether they publish it or not.

Category Fit and Differentiation: The First Filter

Before a buyer evaluates your product, they evaluate whether your product fits an open need in their assortment. This is the first — and most commonly misunderstood — filter in the retailer onboarding process.

Retail buyers manage categories, not individual products. They're looking at a planogram (the shelf layout) with a fixed number of slots. For your product to get a slot, one of three things must be true:

The category is expanding. The buyer has been allocated additional shelf space or an entirely new sub-category is being built. This is the best-case scenario — you're not displacing anyone.

Your product replaces an underperformer. Every planogram has SKUs in the bottom 10% of velocity. Buyers review these quarterly. If your product can credibly promise higher turns than what's currently on shelf, you have a case.

Your product fills a whitespace. The buyer's current assortment doesn't cover a specific need, price point, or customer segment. Maybe they have premium skincare but nothing at a mid-tier clinical price point. Maybe they have conventional snack bars but nothing in the adaptogenic wellness space.

What buyers don't want: another product that looks like what they already have. "We're like [Competitor X] but better" is the fastest way to end a buyer meeting. Buyers aren't looking for incrementally better versions of products they already carry. They're looking for differentiation that brings a new customer to the shelf or captures a need their current assortment misses.

Velocity Proof: Show Demand, Not Potential

Retail buyers are risk managers. Every new SKU they add is a bet — shelf space costs money, and a product that doesn't sell costs them twice (lost revenue from the shelf space, plus the labor to remove it and reset the planogram).

Your job is to de-risk the bet. That means proving demand exists — not just asserting it.

What counts as velocity proof:

DTC sales data. If you're selling 2,000+ units/month through your own site, that's meaningful. It shows real customers paying real money for your product. Bring 6–12 months of sales data, not a single strong month.

Amazon velocity. If you're selling on Amazon with consistent BSR (Best Seller Rank) in your subcategory, that's data a buyer respects. Amazon velocity is the closest public proxy for retail sell-through.

Regional retail performance. Already in 50 independent stores or a regional chain? Bring sell-through data. "We're in 200 doors with 3.2 units/store/week" is the language buyers speak.

Social proof at scale. 100K+ engaged followers, strong UGC (user-generated content), press coverage, influencer traction. This isn't velocity, but it signals demand that hasn't been captured by retail yet — and buyers recognize that as an opportunity.

What doesn't count:

  • "Our product went viral on TikTok" (without sustained sales to match)
  • Friends-and-family sales volume
  • Pre-orders or crowdfunding numbers from 18+ months ago
  • Projections without historical basis

Buyers hear "we'll sell 10,000 units in the first quarter" every day. They believe "we sold 10,000 units last quarter through these channels, and here's the data."

Margin Requirements: The Math the Buyer Runs Before Anything Else

Before a buyer evaluates your brand story, your packaging, or your marketing plan, they check your margin structure. If the math doesn't work, nothing else matters.

Standard retail margin expectations by category:

CategoryTypical Retail MarginWholesale Price (% of MSRP)
Beauty / Skincare50–60%40–50% of MSRP
Food / Beverage35–45%55–65% of MSRP
Wellness / Supplements45–55%45–55% of MSRP
Consumer Electronics30–40%60–70% of MSRP
Home / Lifestyle50–55%45–50% of MSRP

If your product retails at $29.99 and you're in beauty, the buyer expects a wholesale price of $12–$15 (40–50% of MSRP). Your COGS need to support that wholesale price while leaving you a viable margin after freight, compliance costs, and trade spend.

The most common margin mistake: DTC brands price their product for a DTC margin model (70% gross margin on a direct sale) and then discover they can't offer wholesale pricing that works for retail without going below their cost floor.

Run the retail margin math before the buyer meeting, not during it. If your wholesale price doesn't work for the retailer, you have three options: reduce COGS, raise MSRP, or acknowledge that retail isn't the right channel yet.

Operational Readiness: Can You Ship on Time, Every Time?

This is where most emerging brands underperform — and where buyers have the least patience.

A buyer's worst-case scenario isn't a product that doesn't sell. It's a product that sells but can't stay in stock. Empty shelf space is lost revenue, and it makes the buyer look bad internally. Buyers who get burned by out-of-stock emerging brands become buyers who stop taking meetings with emerging brands.

What buyers evaluate for operational readiness:

Fulfillment capability. Do you have a 3PL with retail distribution experience? Can they handle EDI, routing guide compliance, and pallet-level shipments? "We're figuring out our 3PL situation" is a disqualifier.

Inventory depth. Can you fulfill the initial PO and a reorder 8–12 weeks later without a stockout? Buyers want to see that you have the production capacity and cash flow to sustain replenishment.

Lead time management. What's your production lead time? Can you respond to a reorder within 10–16 weeks? If your supply chain runs through a single overseas manufacturer with 20-week lead times, the buyer sees risk.

EDI capability. Are you set up for electronic purchase orders, ASNs, and invoicing? This is a basic requirement for national retailers. Having EDI in place before the buyer meeting signals that you've done the operational homework.

Compliance track record. If you're already in other retail accounts, buyers will ask about your chargeback rate and fill rate. A fill rate below 95% or a chargeback rate above 3% raises flags.

The brands that win these conversations don't just say "we can handle it." They show a capabilities document: their 3PL name and retail accounts served, their EDI provider, their fill rate from existing accounts, and their production lead times. One page. No narrative. Just the operational facts that let a buyer say "this brand won't create problems for me."

Marketing Support: What Buyers Expect You'll Spend

Buyers at major retailers expect emerging brands to invest in driving traffic to their product once it's on shelf. "Build it and they will come" isn't a retail strategy. You need a marketing support plan — and it costs more than most brands expect.

Common marketing support expectations:

Trade spend / promotional allowance. Many retailers expect brands to fund promotional pricing (temporary price reductions, BOGO offers, or percentage-off deals) during key selling periods. Budget 5–15% of wholesale revenue for trade spend in Year 1.

Sampling and demos. In-store product demonstrations or sampling programs are standard expectations in beauty, food, and wellness categories. Costs vary: $150–$300 per demo day per store, plus product cost. A 50-store sampling program runs $15,000–$30,000.

Endcap and secondary placement. Premium shelf placement — endcaps, checkout displays, feature sections — often requires a fee. These vary widely: $500–$5,000 per store per month at major retailers. Not always required for emerging brands, but frequently offered.

Digital marketing support. Some retailers expect brands to run paid digital advertising that drives traffic to the retailer's website or stores. Retailer-specific digital programs (Target Roundel, Walmart Connect, Instacart Ads) are increasingly expected alongside shelf placement.

Co-op advertising. Participation in the retailer's circular, website features, or seasonal marketing campaigns. The retailer handles the creative; you fund a portion.

The total Year 1 marketing support investment for a brand launching in one major retailer typically runs $20,000–$75,000. That's on top of your standard marketing budget. Brands that don't budget for this find themselves either refusing buyer requests (which damages the relationship) or scrambling to fund programs that weren't in the plan.

Insurance and Compliance: The Documentation That Blocks Onboarding

The unglamorous truth: even if a buyer loves your product, the vendor setup process takes 4–12 weeks and requires documentation that most DTC brands don't have ready.

The standard documentation checklist:

  • Product liability insurance: $1M–$2M per occurrence minimum (some retailers require $5M)
  • Certificate of Insurance (COI): naming the specific retailer as additional insured
  • General liability insurance: at retailer-specified minimums
  • Workers' compensation coverage: current and compliant
  • W-9 or W-8BEN: tax documentation
  • FDA registration: required for food, cosmetics, supplements, OTC drugs
  • Third-party certifications: organic (USDA), non-GMO (Non-GMO Project Verified), cruelty-free (Leaping Bunny), fair trade, etc.
  • Safety Data Sheets (SDS): for products containing chemicals or active ingredients
  • Product sell sheets: professional, print-ready, with wholesale pricing and case pack details

The item that delays onboarding most often: insurance. Getting a COI with a specific retailer named as additional insured takes 1–3 weeks through most insurers. If you need to upgrade your coverage limits, add 2–4 weeks. Start the insurance process the moment a buyer expresses interest.

The Timeline: First Meeting to Shelf Placement

Most brands assume that a "yes" from a buyer means they'll be on shelf within a month. The actual timeline from initial buyer meeting to product on shelf:

StageTimeline
Initial buyer meeting / pitchDay 0
Buyer internal review and category analysis2–6 weeks
Line review or assortment decision4–12 weeks (often seasonal)
Vendor setup and documentation4–8 weeks
PO issued2–4 weeks after vendor setup complete
Production and fulfillment of initial order8–16 weeks
Product on shelf2–4 weeks after DC receipt
Total: first meeting to shelf6–12 months

The biggest time surprise: line reviews. Many retailers set their assortments once or twice a year during formal line reviews. If you miss the review window, you wait until the next one. A buyer can love your product in March and not have a slot for it until the September line review for Q1 placement.

Plan accordingly. The conversation you have this quarter may not result in a PO for two or three quarters.

What Most Brands Underestimate

After helping dozens of brands through the retail onboarding process, the pattern is consistent. Here's what gets underestimated:

The timeline. Brands assume weeks. Reality is months. A buyer saying "let's do this" is the beginning of a process, not the end of one.

Trade spend. Marketing support expectations at major retailers run $20,000–$75,000 in Year 1. Brands that budget $5,000 for "in-store marketing" are underprepared.

Margin compression. A brand with 65% DTC gross margins often lands at 35–42% retail gross margins after wholesale pricing, freight, compliance costs, and trade spend. The volume makes up for it at scale. At launch volume, it's a cash flow challenge.

Operational scrutiny. Buyers care more about your ability to ship reliably than your brand story. The most beautiful brand in the pitch deck loses to the operationally prepared brand with a clear fulfillment plan.

Relationship continuity. Getting on shelf is the beginning. Staying on shelf requires sustained performance — fill rates above 95%, velocities that justify the slot, and marketing support that drives turns. Brands that celebrate the PO and then coast lose their shelf placement within 12 months.

The brands that succeed at retail treat it as a channel that requires its own strategy, its own budget, and its own operational infrastructure — not an extension of what they've already built for DTC.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do retail buyers look for in emerging brands?

Retail buyers evaluate six factors: (1) category fit — does the product fill a gap in their current assortment? (2) margin structure — does the wholesale pricing support standard retail margins? (3) velocity proof — is there evidence of real demand? (4) operational readiness — can the brand ship on time, every time? (5) marketing support — will the brand invest in driving traffic to the product on shelf? (6) compliance documentation — is all required insurance, certification, and vendor paperwork ready?

How long does it take to get an emerging brand into retail?

From first buyer meeting to product on shelf, expect 6–12 months. The timeline includes buyer review (2–6 weeks), category/line review decisions (4–12 weeks, often seasonal), vendor setup (4–8 weeks), and production/fulfillment of the initial PO (8–16 weeks). Missing a seasonal line review window can add 3–6 months to the timeline.

What margins do retailers expect from CPG brands?

Margins vary by category. Beauty and skincare buyers typically expect 50–60% retail margin (wholesale price at 40–50% of MSRP). Food and beverage runs 35–45%. Wellness and supplements range 45–55%. Your COGS need to support the wholesale price while leaving viable brand margin after freight, compliance, and trade spend.

How much does it cost to launch in retail as an emerging brand?

Beyond product and inventory costs, budget $15,000–$50,000 for retail readiness (EDI, packaging compliance, insurance, documentation) and $20,000–$75,000 for Year 1 marketing support (trade spend, sampling, promotional programs). Total Year 1 investment beyond inventory: $35,000–$125,000 per major retail account. The brands that underbudget this don't fail on product. They fail on cash flow.

Do I need to be in DTC before approaching retail?

No, but it helps. DTC sales data provides velocity proof that de-risks the buyer's decision. Amazon sales data serves a similar purpose. If you're approaching retail without DTC or Amazon traction, you'll need other demand signals — strong social following, press coverage, successful regional retail performance, or a category whitespace argument that's compelling enough to offset the lack of sales data.

What's the most common reason emerging brands get rejected by retail buyers?

Lack of differentiation. "We're like [Brand X] but better/cleaner/more premium" doesn't give a buyer a reason to add another SKU when they already carry a brand that fills that slot. The brands that get meetings present a clear category whitespace argument: what need exists in the buyer's current assortment that your product uniquely fills?

Frequently Asked Questions

What do retail buyers look for in emerging brands?

Retail buyers evaluate six things: category fit (does this brand fit a gap in the planogram?), margin structure (can the retailer make their required margin?), demand proof (is there evidence customers want this product?), operational readiness (can the brand ship on time, every time?), marketing support (will the brand drive consumers to the shelf?), and compliance documentation (vendor paperwork, insurance, EDI). Brands that get POs address all six. Brands that only address one or two — usually product and brand story — often don't.

What margin do retailers require from emerging brands?

Margin requirements vary by category and channel. Beauty and personal care typically require 50–55% retailer margin, meaning the brand's wholesale price is 45–50% of MSRP. Natural food and beverage typically requires 35–45% retailer margin. Mass market retailers (Target, Walmart) require higher margins than specialty retailers (Whole Foods, Ulta). These numbers compress further when distributor fees (3–7%) or broker fees are involved.

What proof of demand do retailers want to see?

Retailers want to see sell-through data from existing channels. DTC revenue, Amazon BSR rank, and velocity data from smaller retail accounts all count. Awards, press coverage, and social following are supporting signals — not primary evidence. The strongest proof is documented sell-through velocity: how many units per week per location in a comparable retail environment. If you have this, lead with it.

How do you get a product into a major retailer?

The path is: get in front of the right buyer with the right category pitch, have strong proof of demand, pass the margin and compliance review, and be operationally ready to deliver. Practically, this means having a retail-compliant product, proven demand data, a 3PL with retail distribution capability, EDI capability, and a product margin that works at wholesale pricing. Most brands that fail to get shelf space either lack demand proof or aren't operationally ready to deliver at retail scale.

How long does retail vendor onboarding take?

Retailer vendor onboarding typically takes 4–12 weeks after a buyer says yes. This includes completing vendor paperwork, insurance and liability documentation, EDI setup and testing, compliance review of packaging and labeling, and item setup in the retailer's system. Starting this process before the PO is signed — not after — compresses the timeline and reduces the risk of compliance-related delays.
