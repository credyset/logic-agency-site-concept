The Real Cost of Single-Sourcing

Single-sourcing isn't a strategy — it's a default. Most CPG brands don't choose to depend on a single supplier for a critical component. They start with the supplier who could meet their initial MOQ, the relationship works, and inertia keeps them there. By the time the brand is doing $10M in revenue, 100% of their primary packaging comes from one factory in one city in one country.

That works until it doesn't. And when it doesn't, the failure modes are predictable:

Production delays cascade to revenue loss. A factory equipment failure, raw material shortage, or labor disruption that delays production by 4–6 weeks doesn't just delay your inventory. It delays your retail replenishment, your promotional calendar, and potentially your holiday season. For a brand doing $2M in Q4 revenue, a 4-week stockout on the hero SKU can cost $200,000–$500,000 in lost sales — plus the downstream damage to retailer confidence.

Price leverage shifts to the supplier. A supplier who knows they're your only option has zero incentive to hold pricing. Annual price increases of 5–10% are common when the supplier knows you can't walk. Over three years, compounding price creep adds 15–30% to your COGS on that component — cost you wouldn't absorb if you had a qualified alternative.

Quality inconsistency goes unaddressed. Without a benchmark, you can't evaluate whether your supplier's quality is competitive. A defect rate of 2% feels normal until you qualify a second supplier and discover that 0.5% is the industry standard. Single-sourcing eliminates your ability to compare.

Geographic concentration amplifies risk. COVID shutdowns, regional natural disasters, trade policy changes, port congestion — all of these are geographic risks that a single-source, single-geography supply chain can't mitigate. The Suez Canal blockage in 2021 delayed $9.6 billion in global trade per day. Brands with alternative sourcing geographies rerouted. Brands without alternatives waited.

Trigger Points: When It's Time to Diversify

Diversification has a cost — tooling, qualification, management overhead. Not every brand needs it immediately. Here are the trigger points that signal the risk-reward calculation has shifted in favor of diversification.

Revenue Above $5M

Below $5M, your production volumes likely don't justify maintaining two qualified suppliers. The cost of redundancy exceeds the expected cost of disruption. Above $5M, the math inverts. A single-source disruption that costs you one month of revenue is now a $400,000+ event. The $10,000–$15,000 investment in qualifying a secondary supplier is insurance at 3% of potential loss.

SKU Count Above 20

More SKUs means more production complexity, more potential failure points, and longer production queues at a single facility. A supplier running 20+ SKUs for you is scheduling your production around other clients' production. Diversifying allows you to distribute SKU production across suppliers by category or complexity, reducing queue time and lead time risk.

Retail Channel Entry

Retail demands a reliability standard that DTC doesn't. A DTC stockout means lost sales and disappointed customers. A retail stockout means chargebacks, lost shelf space, and potentially a discontinued listing. The consequences of supply failure escalate dramatically when retail accounts are involved, making secondary sourcing for retail-facing components essential.

International Expansion

Selling into new geographies creates both opportunity and obligation. Different regulatory requirements (EU PPWR, California SB 54), different labeling standards, and different consumer expectations may require region-specific packaging. A secondary supplier in or near the target market can reduce lead times, freight costs, and compliance complexity for international orders.

When Your Supplier's Lead Time Exceeds 12 Weeks

Any component with a lead time exceeding 12 weeks represents a forecasting risk. You're ordering based on a demand projection three months out. If the forecast is wrong — in either direction — you either stock out or trap cash in inventory. A secondary supplier with a shorter lead time (domestic manufacturing, for instance) gives you a safety valve for demand surprises.

The 80/20 Dual-Sourcing Model

The 80/20 dual-sourcing framework provides diversification without fragmenting your volume leverage. It's the model we use at Logic Agency for brands that need supply chain resilience without the management burden of a fully distributed supplier network.

80% — Primary Supplier

Your primary supplier handles roughly 80% of production volume. They get the majority of your business, which maintains your volume leverage for pricing. This is typically your most established relationship — the supplier with the best combination of quality, pricing, and reliability.

The primary supplier should be:

  • Your most consistent quality performer
  • Your most price-competitive at volume
  • Geographically accessible for audit and relationship management
  • Capable of handling your current peak production requirements

20% — Backup Supplier

Your secondary supplier handles 20% of production volume. This is your qualified backup — a supplier you've vetted, tooled, and tested at production scale. The 20% allocation serves two purposes: it keeps the supplier relationship warm (a supplier you only call during emergencies won't prioritize you), and it gives you continuous quality comparison data.

The secondary supplier should be:

  • Qualified to your production specifications (same materials, same tolerances, same quality standards)
  • Ideally in a different geographic region than your primary (different country, or at minimum different city/state)
  • Capable of scaling to 50–70% of your production volume within 4–6 weeks if the primary supplier fails

How to Qualify a Secondary Supplier

Qualification is the step most brands skip — they identify a backup supplier in theory but never qualify them in practice. When a disruption hits, they discover that the "backup" hasn't been tooled, hasn't produced to their spec, and can't deliver at the quality standard they need. Qualifying now, before you need them, is the entire point.

The Qualification Process

Week 1–2: Capability Assessment

Share your complete specification package with the prospective supplier. Include: material specifications, dimensional tolerances, finish requirements, print specifications (Pantone codes, registration tolerances), and quality acceptance criteria. Ask for a formal capability review — can they meet every specification? Where do they anticipate challenges?

Week 3–4: Tooling and Setup

For most packaging components, the secondary supplier will need their own tooling — printing plates, die-cuts, molds. Tooling cost varies by component complexity: $500–$3,000 for a folding carton die, $5,000–$25,000 for a rigid box or custom component, and $15,000–$50,000 for glass or plastic molds. This is a sunk cost of diversification, but it's a one-time investment.

Week 5–8: Sample Production

Run a sample production of 500–2,000 units. Inspect against your quality standards. Compare side-by-side with primary supplier output. Document any variance. If the samples pass, you have a qualified secondary supplier. If they don't, provide feedback and run a second sample. If the second sample fails, move on.

Week 9–12: Production Run

Run a small production order (5,000–10,000 units) through normal channels — packed, shipped, received, and inventoried. This tests not just production quality but logistics: packing compliance, freight handling, customs documentation, and receiving accuracy.

Total cost of qualification: $5,000–$15,000 depending on component complexity. Compare that to the cost of a single-source disruption — which typically runs $50,000–$250,000 in lost revenue, expedited freight, and emergency sourcing.

Brands resist diversification because it costs money upfront. Here's the math that changes the conversation.

A single disruption event — which occurs with meaningful probability every 2–3 years for international supply chains — exceeds the total cost of 5+ years of diversification. The insurance math isn't close.

Why 15+ Country Sourcing Capability Matters

At Logic Agency, we manage manufacturing relationships across 15+ countries — not because complexity is a goal, but because geographic diversification is a risk management strategy that serves our clients.

When a brand depends on a single factory in a single country, they're exposed to every risk in that geography: trade policy changes (tariffs, import restrictions), currency fluctuations, port congestion, regional disruptions, and regulatory shifts. A brand with qualified suppliers in China, Vietnam, India, Mexico, and the US can route production to the lowest-risk, lowest-cost geography for each component.

This doesn't mean every brand needs 15 suppliers. It means your operations team — whether internal or fractional — should have the sourcing relationships that give you options when you need them.

The brands that navigated the last five years of supply chain disruption without missing a beat didn't predict the disruptions. They had alternatives ready.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is single-sourcing actually acceptable?

Single-sourcing is acceptable when the component is low-cost, short lead time, and readily available from multiple suppliers on short notice — meaning the cost of disruption is low. It's also acceptable for proprietary components where only one supplier has the capability (custom molds, patented materials). For everything else, single-sourcing is an unmanaged risk, not a strategy.

How do I diversify without losing volume pricing from my primary supplier?

The 80/20 dual-sourcing framework preserves 70% of your volume with the primary supplier — enough to maintain volume pricing at most manufacturers. The per-unit premium on the 20% secondary allocation (typically 3–8% at lower volume) is the explicit cost of insurance. Frame it that way in your supplier conversations: "We're allocating 70% to you and 20% to a secondary for continuity planning." Most suppliers understand and prefer transparency.

What's the right number of suppliers for a CPG brand?

For most brands at $5–30M revenue: two to three qualified suppliers for primary packaging and one to two for secondary packaging. More than that creates management overhead that exceeds the incremental risk reduction. The goal is redundancy on critical components, not a fully distributed supply network.

Does diversification increase or decrease total packaging cost?

In the short term, total cost increases slightly — the secondary supplier's per-unit cost is higher due to lower volume, and qualification has upfront cost. In the medium term (12–24 months), total cost typically decreases. The competitive tension between two qualified suppliers improves pricing from both. Brands with two qualified suppliers pay 5–12% less over a three-year period than brands with a single source, because negotiation leverage shifts to the buyer.

How do I manage quality consistency across multiple suppliers?

Standardized specifications, identical quality acceptance criteria, and regular side-by-side comparison. Every incoming shipment gets the same inspection protocol regardless of source. Variances between suppliers are documented and addressed immediately. The secondary supplier should produce output that's indistinguishable from the primary — if it's not, the qualification process isn't complete.
